Included Bandwidth vs Pay-Per-GB Egress: Which Cloud Pricing Model Is Cheaper?

Daya Shankar
September 5, 2026
16 min read
Included bandwidth and pay-per-GB egress solve different cost problems. Metered egress works well when outbound traffic is low because there is little fixed network cost. Bundled or unmetered bandwidth becomes more attractive as traffic grows, but port speed, fair-use terms and the higher base price still matter. The useful comparison is the monthly break-even point for your real workload.
Quick Answer: Pay-per-GB egress is usually easier to justify for low-traffic workloads because you pay only when data leaves the cloud. Included or unmetered bandwidth can become cheaper once outbound traffic consistently reaches several terabytes per month. If an included-bandwidth plan costs $300 more each month, it breaks even at about 2.6 TB of total monthly traffic against Azure's $0.12/GB Asia tier, or about 3.64 TiB against Google Cloud Standard Tier at $0.085/GiB, after their current free allowances.

Cloud bandwidth pricing usually follows one of two ideas.

One model says:

Pay only for the data you send.

The other says:

Pay for the infrastructure or network capacity, and transfer is included.

Both can be economical.

Both can also become expensive.

The difference appears when the workload moves from hundreds of gigabytes to multiple terabytes every month.

A small API may barely notice egress charges.

A video platform, backup service, software distribution system, AI media application or data pipeline may build its entire cloud economics around them.

That is why the useful question is not:

Which provider has free bandwidth?

It is:

At what traffic level does included bandwidth become cheaper than metered egress for this workload?

For the provider-by-provider rate comparison, first see Cloud Egress Costs in India. This article focuses specifically on the pricing-model decision.

Pricing note: Public rates and provider documentation referenced here were checked on 5 September 2026. Network pricing changes by region, destination, service, routing tier, SKU and commercial agreement. Use the calculations below as decision models, not provider quotes.

There Are More Than Two Bandwidth Pricing Models

“Including bandwidth” can mean several different things.

That distinction matters before comparing providers.

Pricing ModelHow It WorksMain Cost Risk
Pay per GB/GiBOutbound traffic is metered after any free allowanceBill rises directly with traffic
Included transfer quotaPlan includes a fixed amount such as 1 TB; overage may be chargedCost jumps after allowance
Unmetered transferNo per-GB charge under normal termsPort speed, fair-use or sustained-use conditions
Transfer included in SKUData transfer is included as part of a specific VM/GPU/product priceHigher base price and SKU restrictions
Fixed bandwidth capacityBuyer pays for Mbps/Gbps capacity rather than data volumePaying for unused capacity

These models should not be treated as synonyms.

For example, unmetered means volume is not billed per GB. It does not mean the connection has infinite throughput.

Likewise, 1 TB included is not the same as unlimited data transfer.

Pay-Per-GB Egress Works Well When Traffic Is Small

For a low-egress workload, paying for a large fixed bandwidth allowance can be unnecessary.

Examples include:

  • Internal dashboards
  • Development environments
  • Small APIs
  • B2B applications with light payloads
  • Early-stage SaaS products
  • Admin systems
  • Low-volume inference APIs

AWS currently provides 100 GB/month of internet data transfer out free across eligible AWS services and regions, excluding China and GovCloud.

Azure also includes the first 100 GB/month of internet egress. For traffic from Asia over Microsoft’s Premium Global Network, the next 10 TB is currently priced at $0.12/GB.

Google Cloud Standard Tier currently includes the first 200 GiB/month per account, with traffic from regions including Mumbai and Delhi then priced at $0.085/GiB through 10 TiB.

At a few hundred gigabytes per month, the absolute egress bill may remain small compared with compute, databases, engineering or managed-service costs.

That is where metered pricing has a clear advantage:

low traffic produces low network cost.

The Economics Change at Multi-Terabyte Scale

Metered egress scales linearly with outbound data.

That is useful when traffic falls.

It becomes more painful when traffic grows.

Azure Example at 10 TB

Azure’s current Asia Premium Global Network pricing is:

  • First 100 GB: Free
  • Next 10 TB: $0.12/GB

Assume 10,000 GB total monthly internet egress.

Billable transfer:

10,000 GB - 100 GB = 9,900 GB

Estimated egress charge:

9,900 × $0.12 = $1,188/month

That is internet transfer alone.

It does not include NAT Gateway processing, cross-region traffic, CDN, load balancers or taxes.

Google Cloud Standard Tier at 10 TiB

Google Cloud Standard Tier currently lists:

  • First 200 GiB: Free
  • 200 GiB to 10,240 GiB: $0.085/GiB

Assume 10 TiB total:

10 TiB = 10,240 GiB

Billable transfer:

10,240 - 200 = 10,040 GiB

Estimated charge:

10,040 × $0.085 = $853.40/month

This is not an apples-to-apples Azure comparison because:

  • Azure is using decimal GB/TB
  • Google is using GiB/TiB
  • Standard Tier uses a different network-routing model

But the underlying point is clear.

Once traffic reaches several terabytes per month, outbound transfer can become a major infrastructure cost.

The Break-Even Formula Is More Useful Than “Free Egress”

Suppose two otherwise acceptable cloud configurations look like this:

Metered plan: Lower monthly infrastructure price + per-GB egress

Included-bandwidth plan: $300/month higher base price + no ordinary per-GB egress charge

The useful calculation is:

Paid-Traffic Break-Even = Extra Monthly Cost of Included Plan ÷ Metered Egress Rate

Then add any free transfer allowance to determine the total monthly traffic break-even point.

Against Azure Premium Global Network

Extra monthly cost:

$300

Paid egress rate:

$0.12/GB

Paid traffic needed to recover the premium:

$300 ÷ $0.12 = 2,500 GB

Azure also includes 100 GB free.

So total traffic break-even is approximately:

2,500 GB + 100 GB = 2,600 GB

or:

2.6 TB/month

Below roughly 2.6 TB, the metered plan is cheaper under these simplified assumptions.

Above it, the $300 included-bandwidth premium begins to pay for itself.

Against Google Cloud Standard Tier

Extra monthly cost:

$300

Paid rate:

$0.085/GiB

Paid traffic break-even:

$300 ÷ $0.085 = ~3,529 GiB

Add the current 200 GiB free allowance:

3,529 + 200 = ~3,729 GiB

That is approximately:

3.64 TiB/month

The important detail is that a statement such as “the break-even point is 3.5 TiB” can be misleading unless it says whether that refers to billable traffic or total traffic.

Break-Even Points for Different Monthly Premiums

The same formula can be reused for any included-bandwidth plan.

Extra Monthly Cost of Included PlanAzure at $0.12/GBGoogle Standard at $0.085/GiB
$100~0.93 TB total~1.35 TiB total
$200~1.77 TB total~2.49 TiB total
$300~2.60 TB total~3.64 TiB total
$500~4.27 TB total~5.94 TiB total
$1,000~8.43 TB total~11.68 TiB total

These figures include the current free allowance in each example.

They deliberately exclude other differences in compute, storage, NAT, performance, support and tax.

That is why the table should be used as a screening tool, not a purchasing verdict.

Included Bandwidth Changes the Cost of Growth

A metered plan makes network cost variable.

If monthly egress doubles from 2 TB to 4 TB, the egress line rises with it.

An included or unmetered model can behave differently.

AceCloud, for example, currently says bandwidth is unmetered for most normal workloads, with no ordinary per-GB charge for traffic leaving VMs.

Its FAQ adds that sustained CDN-scale traffic in the tens of terabytes per month from a single instance may be discussed during onboarding.

Yotta Shakti uses a different version of the included model.

Its current AI Workspace, AI Lab, GPU, cluster and serverless GPU pricing pages list unlimited data ingress and egress on a number of specific SKUs.

That does not mean every Yotta product has free transfer. The same broader pricing page also lists separate ingress and egress add-ons for other use cases.

The distinction is important:

Included bandwidth can be a product feature, not a provider-wide rule.

Unlimited Transfer Does Not Mean Unlimited Speed

Data volume and network throughput are different measurements.

Data volume answers:

How many TB can I transfer?

Throughput answers:

How quickly can I transfer them?

An unmetered plan can still use a fixed 50 Mbps, 100 Mbps, 500 Mbps or 1 Gbps interface.

The theoretical maximum data moved over a 30-day month is:

Continuous Port SpeedTheoretical Maximum in 30 Days
50 Mbps~16.2 TB
100 Mbps~32.4 TB
200 Mbps~64.8 TB
500 Mbps~162 TB
1 Gbps~324 TB

For example:

50 Mbps × 30 days ≈ 16.2 TB theoretical maximum

That assumes the connection runs continuously at full line rate.

Real applications usually transfer less because of:

  • Protocol overhead
  • TCP behavior
  • Request patterns
  • Congestion
  • Application bottlenecks
  • Shared interfaces
  • Server capacity
  • Packet loss
  • Idle periods
Important: "Unlimited transfer" removes the volume meter. It does not remove the throughput limit. A bandwidth-heavy buyer should always compare both TB/month and Mbps/Gbps.

A Fixed Port Can Be a Natural Cost Control

With pay-per-GB egress, a traffic surge can become a billing surge.

With fixed network capacity, the port itself places an upper bound on how quickly traffic can leave.

That can make spending more predictable.

It can also create a performance ceiling.

Consider a product launch where user demand increases fivefold.

Metered Egress

The platform may continue serving additional traffic if compute and network capacity are available.

The bill scales with usage.

Fixed or Included Bandwidth

The monthly data-transfer bill may remain stable.

But the application can eventually hit:

  • Port saturation
  • Queueing
  • Higher latency
  • Packet loss
  • Throughput limits

Predictability therefore has a trade-off.

A fixed cost is useful only when the included network capacity can support peak demand.

Which Workloads Fit Pay-Per-GB Better?

Pay-per-GB is generally attractive when outbound traffic is:

Low Occasional Easy to forecast Small relative to compute cost Able to benefit from CDN caching

Examples include:

  • Internal SaaS
  • Admin applications
  • Development workloads
  • Small APIs
  • Low-volume B2B systems
  • Text-heavy AI inference
  • Data-processing systems where results remain inside the cloud

In these workloads, paying an extra fixed amount for bandwidth that remains mostly unused may reduce cost efficiency.

Which Workloads Fit Included Bandwidth Better?

Included, bundled or unmetered bandwidth becomes more interesting when outbound traffic is:

High Growing Bursty Difficult to forecast A large share of total cloud cost

Examples include:

  • Video delivery
  • Large software downloads
  • Image-generation platforms
  • Video AI
  • Backup restores
  • Dataset distribution
  • CDN origin workloads
  • Customer file delivery
  • Public artifact repositories

For these workloads, eliminating a per-GB charge can materially change unit economics.

Four Example Workloads

WorkloadMonthly Egress PatternPricing Model Worth Testing First
Internal dashboard<100 GBMetered
Small SaaS API200 GB–1 TBMetered
Growing media platform3–20 TBCompare break-even carefully
Video / dataset distribution20 TB+Included/unmetered deserves serious testing

These are directional ranges rather than universal rules.

A workload with 20 TB of traffic might still belong on a hyperscaler if its CDN, global backbone, security, managed services or engineering ecosystem produces a lower total cost.

Predictability Has Economic Value Too

Cloud cost is not only about the lowest expected invoice.

It is also about variance.

Assume two providers have the same expected annual infrastructure cost.

One fluctuates between:

₹3 lakh and ₹8 lakh per month

depending on traffic.

The other stays close to:

₹5 lakh per month

because network transfer is bundled.

Finance, procurement and engineering may value the second model even if its average bill is slightly higher.

Predictability can improve:

  • Budgeting
  • Gross-margin forecasting
  • Customer pricing
  • Capacity planning
  • Reserved-spend decisions
  • Investor forecasting
  • Internal cost allocation

This becomes especially important for SaaS companies whose own customer pricing is fixed while infrastructure traffic varies.

But Included Bandwidth Can Be Wasted

Bundled bandwidth is not automatically economical.

If a plan costs more because it includes generous network capacity and the workload uses almost none of it, the organization is effectively paying for idle bandwidth.

That is similar to buying an oversized VM.

The goal is not to maximize what is included.

The goal is to minimize:

Total cost for the capacity actually required.

This is why the break-even point matters.

Do Not Compare Egress Without NAT and Other Network Charges

A metered cloud can have more than one per-GB network meter.

AWS and Azure both document managed NAT Gateway charges that can sit on top of ordinary internet transfer.

Other architecture components can include:

  • Cross-zone transfer
  • Inter-region transfer
  • Load balancers
  • Firewalls
  • Transit gateways
  • Public IPs
  • CDN
  • Private connectivity

A better formula is:

Effective Outbound Cost = Internet Egress + NAT + Cross-Zone + Inter-Region + Gateway Processing + CDN/Edge Charges

An included-bandwidth plan should be checked in the same way.

“No egress fee” does not necessarily mean that load balancers, firewalls, public IPs or private links are free.

For the detailed provider comparison, see Cloud Egress Costs in India.

CDN Can Move the Break-Even Point

Suppose an application sends 10 TB/month of static media.

Without a CDN, most of that traffic may come directly from origin infrastructure.

With a high cache-hit ratio, the origin may serve only a fraction of the total.

That can reduce the value of switching clouds purely to avoid origin egress.

The relevant calculation becomes:

Origin Egress + CDN Requests + CDN Delivery

rather than:

Origin Egress Alone

A CDN can also improve latency for geographically distributed users.

That performance consideration matters when comparing a lower-cost network path with a premium backbone.

The Cloud Latency in India 2026 article covers why network cost and network quality should be tested together.

Included Bandwidth Needs a Procurement Checklist

Before treating an included-bandwidth plan as predictable, confirm what “included” actually means.

  1. Is the transfer allowance measured in GB, GiB, TB or TiB?
  2. Is data volume truly unmetered?
  3. What is the port speed?
  4. Is the port dedicated or shared?
  5. Are ingress and egress both included?
  6. Does the rule apply to every service or only the selected SKU?
  7. Are cross-region transfers included?
  8. Is object-storage egress included?
  9. Does CDN traffic have separate pricing?
  10. Are NAT or gateway processing charges separate?
  11. Is sustained high-volume traffic subject to fair-use terms?
  12. Can the provider throttle traffic?
  13. What happens during traffic spikes?
  14. Are there overage charges after a hidden or stated threshold?
  15. Are bandwidth upgrades available?
  16. Does the contract define sustained-use limits?
  17. Is the network SLA different from the compute SLA?
  18. What is the expected cost at 5 TB, 20 TB and 100 TB/month?

For the broader procurement process, use the Cloud Provider Selection Checklist for India.

A Simple Decision Framework

Choose Metered Egress When

Traffic is low, bandwidth demand is uncertain on the low side, and paying for unused network capacity would outweigh the expected transfer bill.

Choose Included Bandwidth When

Traffic consistently exceeds the break-even point and the included throughput is sufficient for peak demand without problematic fair-use restrictions.

Benchmark Both When

Traffic sits near the break-even point, changes rapidly, or network performance and managed services are as important as the transfer price.

A Better Way to Compare the Two Models

Use this sequence.

Step 1: Measure Current Outbound Traffic

Record at least three months of:

  • Total internet egress
  • Peak Mbps/Gbps
  • 95th-percentile throughput if available
  • Traffic by workload
  • Traffic by destination

Step 2: Forecast Growth

Model:

  • Normal month
  • High-growth month
  • Traffic spike
  • 12-month expected volume

Step 3: Calculate Metered Cost

Use:

Free Allowance + Paid Egress Tiers + NAT + Other Network Charges

Step 4: Calculate Included-Bandwidth Cost

Use:

Extra Base Price + Network Add-ons + Required Port Upgrade

Step 5: Find the Break-Even Point

Use:

Extra Fixed Monthly Cost ÷ Metered Egress Rate

Then add any free allowance.

Step 6: Test Performance

Compare:

  • p50 latency
  • p95 latency
  • p99 latency
  • Throughput
  • Packet loss
  • Jitter
  • Geographic coverage

Step 7: Check Commercial Terms

Confirm:

  • Fair-use policy
  • Overage
  • Port limit
  • SLA
  • Contract length
  • Upgrade path
  • Cancellation terms

Only then is the bandwidth comparison complete.

FAQs

Is included bandwidth always cheaper than pay-per-GB egress?

No. Included bandwidth usually has a fixed cost built into the plan. If outbound traffic is low, a metered provider can be cheaper because little egress is billed. Included bandwidth becomes more attractive when traffic regularly exceeds the plan's break-even point.

How do I calculate the bandwidth break-even point?

Divide the extra monthly cost of the included-bandwidth plan by the metered egress rate. Then add any free egress allowance to calculate the total monthly traffic level where the two simplified costs become equal.

What is the break-even point for a $300 premium against Azure's $0.12/GB rate?

The $300 premium equals 2,500 GB of paid egress at $0.12/GB. After adding Azure's first 100 GB free, the simplified total traffic break-even is about 2,600 GB, or 2.6 TB per month.

What is the break-even point for a $300 premium against Google Cloud Standard Tier?

At $0.085/GiB, $300 buys about 3,529 GiB of paid traffic. Adding the current 200 GiB free allowance gives roughly 3,729 GiB total, or about 3.64 TiB per month.

Does unlimited bandwidth mean unlimited speed?

No. An unmetered or unlimited-transfer service can still have a fixed port speed such as 50 Mbps, 100 Mbps or 1 Gbps. That port limits how quickly data can move even when there is no per-GB charge.

How much data can a 50 Mbps connection transfer in one month?

A 50 Mbps connection running continuously at full line rate for 30 days can theoretically transfer about 16.2 TB. Real usable transfer is lower because applications do not sustain perfect line-rate utilization and network overhead also applies.

Does AceCloud include egress?

AceCloud currently says bandwidth is unmetered for most normal workloads and that standard SaaS, API and web traffic is not separately billed per GB. Its FAQ notes that sustained CDN-scale traffic in the tens of terabytes per month from a single instance may be discussed during onboarding.

Does Yotta Shakti include bandwidth?

Several current Shakti AI Workspace, GPU, AI Lab and cluster SKUs state that data transfer is included or unlimited. However, Yotta also publishes separate transfer add-on pricing for other services, so buyers should verify the exact SKU rather than assume a provider-wide rule.

Which pricing model is better for a video or media platform?

Included or unmetered bandwidth can become attractive because media workloads can produce large and unpredictable outbound volumes. However, CDN pricing, cache hit ratio, port throughput and global delivery performance should be included before deciding.

Which pricing model is better for a small SaaS application?

Pay-per-GB is often simpler for a small SaaS workload with low outbound traffic. If the application sends only a few hundred gigabytes per month, the egress bill may be lower than the fixed premium required for a large included-bandwidth plan.

Final Takeaway

Included bandwidth is not automatically cheaper.

Pay-per-GB egress is not automatically more expensive.

The decision depends on three numbers:

Monthly Traffic + Metered Egress Rate + Extra Cost of the Included Plan

Then add the constraints that pricing tables do not capture:

Port Speed + Fair-Use Terms + Network Performance + Other Network Charges

For low-egress workloads, metered pricing often remains the rational choice.

For workloads consistently moving several terabytes per month, included or unmetered bandwidth deserves serious evaluation.

And for workloads near the break-even point, neither headline is enough.

Calculate the threshold, test the network, and compare the total production architecture.
